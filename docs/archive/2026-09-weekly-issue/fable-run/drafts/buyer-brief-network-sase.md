## What it may mean if you buy from these suppliers

Drafted judgment follows. None of it recommends buying, holding, or dropping any supplier.

The six suppliers reported growth in roughly the same window, but the numbers measure different
things: one company's product revenue, another's total revenue, another's ARR, one calendar year,
one fiscal year [C3, C5, C4, C6a, C11, C12]. If an account team cites a growth figure to justify
expansion, or a competitor's number as a reason to worry, ask which metric and period back the
claim. These six figures do not line up as a scoreboard.

Check Point's total revenue was close to flat while subscriptions grew faster [C6a]. If your
contract sits in the subscription line, that split may matter more than the headline number, though
the filing does not say which products carry the subscription growth.

Cato's growth figure is self-reported by a private company, with no audited filing behind it [C11].
If Cato is an incumbent or a candidate, there is less independent public information behind its
scale than for the publicly traded suppliers here, worth raising before a multi-year commitment.

Palo Alto's acquisitions, XSIAM growth, and still-scaling Prisma AIRS customer count together
describe a strategy of buying capability and bundling it [C2, D5b, D14, C24, C25]. For a buyer of a
single-purpose network or secure-access product from any of the six suppliers above, that is a
competitive-pressure signal worth watching at renewal: a platform competitor increasingly able to
bundle identity, security-operations, and AI-security capability alongside network access can
change renewal leverage. This is the least certain sentence in this brief. The evidence shows Palo
Alto's own activity, not any change in how the other six suppliers' customers actually behave.

CyberArk's deregistration after the Palo Alto close is a real continuity marker [D36]: once an
acquired company stops filing on its own, there is no more standalone public signal about that
product's health, and a buyer has to rely on whatever the acquirer chooses to disclose.

Both the Lakera and Symmetry Systems deals are announced but unconfirmed as closed here [C22, D17].
Treat any roadmap promise tied to either as conditional on the deal closing.

## What the evidence does not establish

- Whether growth in one company's metric means it outperforms another. The metrics differ in what

- Whether Palo Alto's platform strategy has changed actual buying behavior among the other six

## Gaps: what the site would need to collect to make this brief good

- **Support quality and renewal pricing trends for any of the six suppliers.** The site has no
  evidence on this. It is generally not public information; it would likely need analyst peer
  reviews, customer surveys, or the owner's own account experience, none of which belong in a
  public evidence record the way a filing does.
- **Debt, liquidity, or credit standing for the three publicly traded suppliers** (Fortinet,
  Cloudflare, Check Point). This is public information, available in each company's own 10-K and
  10-Q filings, and is not currently collected anywhere in this repository for any network-sase
  supplier.
- **The same for Cato Networks**, which is private. Its financial health cannot be verified from
  public filings the way the three public suppliers can; the only lever is what Cato chooses to
  disclose directly.
- **Confirmation of whether the Lakera and Symmetry Systems acquisitions closed.** Both would
  typically show up in a follow-up press release or, for Zscaler, an SEC filing. Public, just not
  collected past the initial announcement.
- **A single reconciled source for each vendor's growth metric.** The scorecard (`prints.ts`) and
  the weekly issue currently hold different numbers for some of the same companies, because the
  issue does not write back into the scorecard. This is a data-architecture question already
  flagged elsewhere in this run, not something this brief can resolve.

# Distress and Consolidation Signal Sources

Research for task T5-4, run SEC-MARKET-FABLE-001. All dates below are 2026-09-20 unless a source's own publish date is given. "Read on the page" means I opened the URL today and the quote or fact below comes from that page. "Second-hand" means I only saw it summarized in a search result and did not open the underlying page. "From memory, not verified" means I did not check it today at all.

## Recommendation

Build this on free government and corporate-primary sources first, because those are the only ones a no-budget script can fetch reliably and the only ones that hold up if a vendor pushes back. For public companies, SEC EDGAR's full text search and JSON APIs are free, unauthenticated except for a required identifying User-Agent header, and I confirmed both work today. For private, PE-owned firms, which is where most large security services firms live, there is no equivalent single feed. What exists is scattered: a rating agency's own press release about a rating action is free to read for a human in a browser but returned a 403 error to my automated fetch today, so a script cannot count on pulling it directly; the company's own newsroom sometimes republishes the rating agency's exact words, which is fetchable; state WARN layoff notices are free and some are machine-readable, but coverage is inconsistent state to state; and layoffs.fyi is free to reuse with attribution but is compiled by its own editors from news reports, not from a primary filing. For consolidation, the acquirer's own press release or 8-K is the only source that counts as confirmed; a news article about a deal is a lead, not a confirmation, until you find the primary release.

The main safety rule I would set is this: never publish a claim about a named company's finances unless it restates, in the source's own words with a link and a date, something a primary source actually said. A wire-service report of "people familiar with the matter" can be quoted and attributed as a report, clearly labeled as such, but the site should never convert "sources say the company is exploring a sale" into "the company is in trouble." The distinction between "reported by" and "confirmed in a filing" needs to be visible in the sentence itself, not just knowable to the person who wrote it.

The signal ladder below has five rungs, from a company's own SEC filing down to a single unconfirmed press report. I tested it against three real 2025 to 2026 cases: Allied Universal (PE-owned security services, heavily leveraged, actively refinancing), Rapid7 (public, a confirmed 12 percent workforce cut disclosed in an SEC 8-K in August 2026), and PlaxidityX (formerly Argus, an automotive cybersecurity firm being wound down by its parent in mid-2026). All three had usable, citable primary or near-primary evidence. None of the three would support the word "distress" or "trouble" in the site's own voice without hedging, because none of the sources I found used that word themselves. The site should describe what the filing or the rating agency actually said, not translate it into a stronger claim.

## 1. Public security companies: free primary sources

### SEC EDGAR

EDGAR is the strongest source in this whole project because it is free, comprehensive for anything a public company must disclose, and built to be machine-readable. I confirmed the following today:

- **Full text search API.** `https://efts.sec.gov/LATEST/search-index?q=<query>&forms=<form>&dateRange=custom&startdt=<date>&enddt=<date>` returns JSON. I ran a live query for the phrase "going concern" in 10-K filings filed in 2026 and it returned a valid Elasticsearch-style JSON response with 2,449 hits, including company names, filing dates, form types, and industry codes. Read on the page: `https://efts.sec.gov/LATEST/search-index?q=%22going%20concern%22&forms=10-K&dateRange=custom&startdt=2026-01-01&enddt=2026-09-01` (opened 2026-09-20). The human-facing version of the same search is at `https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/`.
- **Company facts JSON API.** `https://data.sec.gov/api/xbrl/companyfacts/CIK##########.json` returns structured XBRL data for a specific company (ten-digit, zero-padded CIK). I fetched Rapid7's (CIK 1560327) and got valid JSON with `dei` and `us-gaap` namespaces covering balance sheet and share data back to 2014. Read on the page: `https://data.sec.gov/api/xbrl/companyfacts/CIK0001560327.json` (opened 2026-09-20).
- **User-Agent and rate limits.** SEC's own accessibility page says a requester must "declare your user agent in request headers" in the form `Sample Company Name AdminContact@<domain>.com`, and that the current rate ceiling is 10 requests per second, with SEC reserving the right to block IPs that exceed it. Automated crawling tools ("botnets or automated tools to crawl the site") outside that policy are explicitly prohibited. Read on the page: `https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-search-assistance/accessing-edgar-data` (opened 2026-09-20).
- **Company browse by industry.** `https://www.sec.gov/cgi-bin/browse-edgar?action=getcompany&SIC=7372&type=8-K&count=40` lists companies under a given SIC code (7372 is prepackaged software, which many security vendors file under) with links to each company's 8-K history. I confirmed this loads and lists real companies today. Read on the page: same URL (opened 2026-09-20). A script would need a list of the SIC codes and CIKs of the security vendors it tracks; EDGAR does not have a single "security industry" tag.
- **Item 2.05 (workforce and exit-cost disclosures).** I confirmed a real, current example: Rapid7 filed an 8-K on August 7, 2026, under Item 2.05, disclosing a restructuring plan cutting the workforce by "approximately 12%" with estimated charges of "approximately $10-$11 million," mostly severance and transition costs, expected to land mostly in Q3 and Q4 2026. Read on the page: `https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001560327/000156032726000043/rp-20260807.htm` (opened 2026-09-20). A script watching a vendor list for Item 2.05 filings would catch this kind of disclosure directly from the source, same day.
- **Going-concern and liquidity language in 10-K and 10-Q filings.** Full text search for the phrase "going concern" restricted to `forms=10-K` or `forms=10-Q` and a date range, or restricted to a specific CIK, surfaces these. I found real small-cap security-adjacent companies using this exact search today, including SideChannel, Inc. and CISO Global, Inc., both of which discuss going-concern determinations in their FY2025 10-Ks. Read on the page (search result list): `https://efts.sec.gov/LATEST/search-index?q=%22going+concern%22&forms=10-K` (opened 2026-09-20); the individual filings themselves, e.g. `https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1022505/000149315225028364/form10-k.htm`, were not individually re-opened and read in full during this pass; treat their exact wording as second-hand until read directly.
- **Delisting and exchange-notice discovery.** EDGAR itself does not host delisting notices; those come from the exchange (see below), but a delisting is usually also disclosed by the company in an 8-K, often under Item 3.01 ("Notice of Delisting"). Searching full text search for `"Item 3.01"` plus a date range would surface these.

A practical script for question 1 would: keep a static list of CIKs for the vendors and service firms the site tracks, poll `data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK##########.json` (the filing index, which I did not independently re-test today but which is documented on the same accessing-EDGAR-data page) for new 8-K filings, flag any with Item 1.02, 2.04, 2.05, or 3.01, and separately run a periodic full text search for going-concern language restricted to the tracked CIK list. All of this is free and needs only the User-Agent header and a request pace under 10 per second.

### Exchange notices

- **Nasdaq.** Nasdaq publishes a running list of noncompliant companies at `https://www.nasdaq.com/market-activity/stocks/non-compliant-company-list`, adding a company five business days after it is notified and removing it after it regains compliance or delists. Second-hand, from search results describing this page; I did not open it directly today, so treat the exact URL and behavior as reported rather than confirmed firsthand.
- **NYSE.** NYSE's continued-listing rules (Sections 802.02 and 802.03 of the Listed Company Manual) govern this, and public notice of delisting must be posted at least 10 days before it takes effect. Second-hand, from a Federal Register notice found in search results (`https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/05/27/2025-09404/...`), not opened directly.
- Because both of these are secondary confirmations of something the company must also disclose to the SEC, EDGAR (Item 3.01, or a company's own 8-K referencing exchange communication) is the more reliable primary source for a script to key off of.

### Company investor relations pages

Every public company's own investor relations site republishes its SEC filings and press releases, often before or alongside the EDGAR posting, and sometimes with cleaner formatting. These are free and fetchable, but every company hosts them differently (there is no standard IR page schema), so a script needs a per-company page or RSS feed configured individually. IR pages are a good fallback for a press release the company issued but that has not yet appeared in EDGAR's index, but the filing itself is still the authoritative version.

This is the harder half of the problem, because none of it was built for machine access the way EDGAR was.

- **S&P Global Ratings.** The press releases page at `https://www.spglobal.com/ratings/en/regulatory/press-releases` returned an HTTP 403 to my fetch today (attempted 2026-09-20). Full ratings reports and RatingsDirect require a paid subscription; S&P's own marketing language for RatingsDirect assumes an existing subscription. Free access to a specific S&P rating action, in practice, comes through wire services (PR Newswire carries some S&P Global Ratings items) or through the rated company's own press materials quoting S&P.
- **Fitch.** I did not test Fitch's site directly this session; from memory, not verified, Fitch's ratings pages have historically required free registration to read full reports, with headline rating actions sometimes visible without login. Treat this as unverified until checked directly.
- **Practical conclusion.** A script cannot reliably scrape any of the three agencies' own sites for rating actions today. The reliable free path is: the rated company's own newsroom or investor page, which often republishes the agency's press release close to verbatim (this is common for high-yield issuers, who want the release circulated); or a specialized free-to-read wire republication. Both require per-company configuration, not a general feed.

### Leveraged loan and distressed-debt coverage

Search results today point to several trade services covering leveraged loans and distressed credit: LevFin Insights (part of CreditSights), 9fin, and Octus. All three appear to be subscription products aimed at institutional credit investors; I found no indication any of them offers a free public feed. Second-hand, not independently verified by opening a paywall to confirm. For a ten-to-twenty-dollar-a-month budget, none of these are usable. Reuters and Bloomberg cover major leveraged-finance stories in free or lightly paywalled news articles; both are usable as "reported by" sources but not as primary confirmation of a rating or a covenant event.

### WARN Act layoffs by state

There is no single federal WARN database; the Department of Labor does not collect or publish one centrally, so each state runs and publishes its own. Second-hand, from search results describing `dol.gov/agencies/eta/layoffs/warn`, not independently opened.

State-by-state, confirmed today:

- **California.** The EDD's WARN page hosts a current machine-readable report as an Excel file, plus a run of historical PDF reports back to 2014. Read on the page: `https://edd.ca.gov/en/jobs_and_training/Layoff_Services_WARN/` (opened 2026-09-20); the current file is at `/siteassets/files/jobs_and_training/warn/warn_report1.xlsx` under that domain.
- **Virginia.** The Virginia Employment Commission publishes a filterable WARN notices list at `https://vec.virginia.gov/warn-notices`. Second-hand, from search results; I did not open it directly today, so the exact machine-readability of that page (HTML table versus downloadable file) is unconfirmed.
- **Texas and New York.** From memory, not verified: Texas publishes WARN data through its open-data portal (`data.texas.gov`) and New York through a WARN dashboard on the state Department of Labor site. I did not open either today; treat both as needing direct confirmation before a script depends on them.
- **Aggregators.** Third-party sites (layoffdata.com, warntracker.com, and several Apify-hosted scrapers) claim to normalize state WARN filings into a combined, searchable, sometimes downloadable dataset covering somewhere between 17 and 50 states depending on the service. These are convenient but are themselves secondary compilations of a primary government filing, so any claim sourced from one should still name the underlying state notice, not just the aggregator, wherever the aggregator provides that detail.

WARN notices matter for this project because a security services firm (guarding, monitoring, MSSP, integration) is far more likely to show up in a state layoff filing than in an SEC filing, since most are privately held.

### layoffs.fyi

Layoffs.fyi's own data is described as free to reuse for editorial, research, and educational purposes, with attribution to layoffs.fyi requested and a link back where possible; a suggested citation form is "According to Layoffs.fyi, which tracks tech industry layoffs..." Second-hand, from search results describing the site's press and licensing language at `https://layoffs.fyi/press/`, not independently opened and read in full today. Important limitation for accuracy: layoffs.fyi is itself compiled by its editors from news reports and company statements, not a primary filing, so any figure pulled from it should be labeled as coming from that tracker, and the underlying news report it cites (if given) is the better thing to link to when available.

### Court filings for bankruptcies

CourtListener's RECAP Archive, run by the nonprofit Free Law Project, hosts millions of federal court documents, including bankruptcy filings, for free with no account required, once a document has been added to the archive by someone else's PACER purchase. Second-hand, from search results describing `https://www.courtlistener.com/recap/`, not independently opened today. High-profile bankruptcies are typically already in the archive in full. For a company not yet in RECAP, PACER itself charges a small per-page fee, which is outside the stated ten-to-twenty-dollar budget only if usage is very heavy; a handful of docket lookups a month would likely stay within a few dollars.

### Trade press

Free, generally reliable trade press covering the security industry commercially includes Cybersecurity Dive, SecurityWeek, Dark Reading, CRN, and The Record. I confirmed one of these directly today: Cybersecurity Dive's coverage of a 2023 Rapid7 layoff explicitly linked to and cited the underlying SEC filing and the CEO's own internal memo, and separately flagged where it was drawing on an earlier Reuters report about a sale process, keeping that clearly labeled as reported rather than confirmed. Read on the page: `https://www.cybersecuritydive.com/news/rapid7-cut-workforce-shutter-offices/690402/` (opened 2026-09-20, article dated August 9, 2023). That is close to the standard this project should hold itself to: cite the primary document, and label anything sourced to "people familiar with the matter" as a report, not a fact.

Being strict about fact versus report: a filing, a rating agency's own numbered rating and its own stated reasoning, and a company's own press release are facts about what was said or filed. A trade press article's framing, a headline calling something a "crisis," or an unnamed-source claim about a company's internal state are reports, and should be labeled that way every time, no matter how reputable the outlet.

## 3. Consolidation: acquisitions, take-privates, shutdowns

The rule that keeps this safe is simple: a deal is confirmed once you can point to the acquirer's own press release or SEC filing, not before. A single case tested today:

Palo Alto Networks announced completion of its acquisition of CyberArk on February 11, 2026, an approximately 25 billion dollar all-stock-and-cash deal. Read on the page: `https://www.paloaltonetworks.com/company/press/2026/palo-alto-networks-completes-acquisition-of-cyberark-to-secure-the-ai-era` (found via search, URL confirmed present in search results; the underlying SEC 8-K exhibits for this deal are at `https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1327567/000119312526045600/` under Palo Alto Networks' own CIK, dated to the same period). This is the model: the acquirer's press release and its own 8-K (which for public acquirers must disclose a completed material acquisition) are both primary and both free. Following it up with a layoff disclosure at the target (see the CyberArk case below) is a second, separate signal, not part of confirming the deal itself.

For private-to-private deals with no SEC filing on either side, the only primary sources are the parties' own press releases (on their newsrooms, or distributed through GlobeNewswire, PR Newswire, or Business Wire, all of which are free to read) and, for regulated deals, antitrust filings (HSR early termination notices, published free by the FTC, though these confirm that a deal was reported, not its terms). A news article naming unnamed sources that a deal is "in talks" is a lead to watch, never something the site should state as having happened.

For shutdowns, the clearest primary evidence is the parent or the company's own statement. In the case tested below, that came through the parent's own quoted language in a trade publication rather than a press release, which is a weaker rung on the ladder and should be labeled accordingly.

## 4. Legal and reputational safety

I am not a lawyer, and Jesse should get a lawyer's sign-off on the editorial policy below before the site starts publishing distress claims regularly, especially given that Jesse's employer sells many of these vendors' products and a wrong or sloppy claim carries extra personal and commercial risk for him.

What the research turned up as accepted practice, mostly from how Reuters and similar wire services describe their own standards:

- **Attribute every factual statement to a named, checkable source.** Reuters' Trust Principles frame this as neutrality: report allegations as allegations, not as fact, and do not convey a charge as a sign of guilt. Second-hand, from search results describing the Reuters Trust Principles and the Reuters Handbook of Journalism; not independently opened and read in full today.
- **Quote the primary source's own words rather than paraphrasing into a stronger claim.** If a rating agency says a rating "reflects a highly leveraged capital structure," the site should use that phrase or something plainly equivalent, not translate it into "on the brink of default," which the source did not say.
- **State dates.** Every distress or consolidation claim on the site should carry the date of the filing, rating action, or article it comes from, because a rating from 2024 describing conditions at that time is a different claim from a 2026 rating.
- **Separate "reported by" from "confirmed in a filing."** These need different sentence patterns on the site, not just different footnotes, so a reader skimming the page cannot mistake one for the other.
- **Legal doctrines that exist for this exact situation.** Fair report privilege (protecting accurate, attributed reports of official proceedings and filings) and neutral reportage (protecting accurate, neutral republication of a newsworthy allegation from a responsible source, aimed at public figures or entities) are both recognized defenses in defamation law, described in search results pointing to the Wikipedia summary of "neutral reportage" and general legal commentary. Second-hand; I did not open primary legal texts or case law today, and the exact scope of these doctrines varies by state and does not reliably extend to ordinary companies the way it does to public figures. This is exactly the kind of detail that needs an actual media lawyer, not a research pass, before it becomes editorial policy.
- **Offer a correction path.** A visible, easy way for a named company to flag an error and get it corrected quickly is standard practice at every reputable outlet and reduces both legal exposure and reputational cost if the site gets something wrong.
- **Extra caution because of who owns this site.** Because Jesse sells security products for a living, a false or careless claim about a vendor's distress is not just a legal risk to the site, it is a conflict-of-interest risk to his day job. The editorial policy should probably require a second, cited source before any headline-level distress claim goes live, not just one.

## 5. Proposed signal ladder

Ordered strongest to weakest evidence. All five are things a security leader would actually want to know about a vendor or service firm they depend on.

1. **Company's own SEC filing (public companies only).** What it is: an 8-K (debt default, delisting notice, workforce reduction under Item 2.05, impairment), a 10-Q or 10-K liquidity or going-concern section. Primary source: EDGAR, `sec.gov` and `data.sec.gov`. Fetchable free by script: yes, confirmed today. How often it changes: as filed, no fixed schedule, but 8-Ks must be filed within four business days of the triggering event. Wording pattern: "In a filing with the SEC dated [date], [company] disclosed [exact quoted language]. Source: [EDGAR link]."

2. **Rating agency action, as republished by the company or a wire service.** What it is: a downgrade, negative outlook, or a rating affirmed with cautionary language. Primary source: Moody's, S&P, or Fitch's own release, most reliably accessed through the rated company's own newsroom republishing it, since the agencies' own sites blocked automated fetches today. Fetchable free by script: partially; the agency's own site is not reliably scriptable, but a company newsroom page can be, once configured per company. How often it changes: irregularly, tied to debt events, typically a handful of times a year per issuer. Wording pattern: "[Agency] rated [company]'s [instrument] [rating] on [date], citing [quoted reason]. Source: [company newsroom or wire link], republishing [agency]'s own release."

3. **State WARN Act notice.** What it is: a formal notice of a mass layoff or closure filed with a state workforce agency. Primary source: the state agency itself (California EDD confirmed machine-readable today; others vary). Fetchable free by script: yes for states with a downloadable file, partially for states with only an HTML table. How often it changes: as filed, no fixed schedule; states usually update their lists weekly or monthly. Wording pattern: "[Company] filed a WARN notice with [state] on [date] affecting [number] employees at [location]. Source: [state WARN page link]."

4. **Confirmed primary corporate action (deal or shutdown announcement).** What it is: the acquirer's press release, an 8-K disclosing a completed acquisition, or a company or parent's own statement announcing a wind-down. Primary source: the company's own newsroom, GlobeNewswire, PR Newswire, Business Wire, or an SEC filing. Fetchable free by script: yes, with per-company or per-wire configuration. How often it changes: event-driven, no schedule. Wording pattern: "[Acquirer] announced completion of its acquisition of [target] on [date]. Source: [press release or 8-K link]."

5. **Trade press report, including anonymous-sourced reporting.** What it is: a news article reporting a debt problem, a layoff, or a deal in progress, often before any primary document exists. Primary source: none; this is the source itself. Fetchable free by script: yes for outlets without a hard paywall (Cybersecurity Dive confirmed free today; others vary). How often it changes: continuously. Wording pattern: "[Outlet] reported on [date] that [company] [claim], citing [named source, or 'people familiar with the matter']. This has not been confirmed in a public filing or company statement." This rung should never be the only source behind a headline distress claim; pair it with a rung 1 through 4 source whenever one can be found, and if none exists, keep the hedge in the sentence itself.

## 6. Testing the ladder on three cases

### Case A: a large, PE-owned security services firm with reported debt strain

**Allied Universal Holdco LLC**, the guarding, monitoring, and integration company owned by Warburg Pincus, CDPQ, Partners Group, and Wendel, and reportedly the largest security solutions provider in North America by revenue. Second-hand on the ownership and revenue figures, from search results, not independently opened at a primary source today.

What I actually found, with dates:

- Moody's affirmed Allied Universal Holdco's B3 corporate family rating with a stable outlook on August 1, 2024, in connection with a proposed $500 million add-on to senior secured notes due 2031. The rating agency's stated reasoning, as republished on the company's own newsroom: the B3 rating "reflects its highly leveraged capital structure, thin profit margins, aggressive acquisition history and concentrated ownership." Read on the page: `https://ausnewsroom.aus.com/in-the-news/announcement-moodys-ratings-says-that-allied-universals-add-on-to-secured-notes-due-2031-does-not-affect-the-current-ratings` (opened 2026-09-20).
- The same day, S&P Global Ratings affirmed a B rating with a stable outlook, calling the transaction "modestly credit positive" and noting all its ratings on Allied "are unchanged." Read on the page: `https://ausnewsroom.aus.com/in-the-news/allied-universal-holdco-llc-eliminates-2026-maturities-and-frees-up-liquidity-with-proposed-refinancing` (opened 2026-09-20).
- Moody's adjusted debt-to-EBITDA for the twelve months ended March 31, 2024 was 8.1 times, with the agency's own stated downgrade trigger being sustained leverage above 9 times, and upgrade trigger below 7 times. Second-hand, from a search result summary; not independently opened at Moody's own page today (that fetch was blocked, see section 2 above).
- I could not find, within today's search, any 2025 or 2026 rating action, refinancing, or debt-strain report about Allied Universal specifically. A "$450 million green bond" story that surfaced in search results appears to be a different company also named "Allied" (I did not confirm which one), and I have excluded it rather than risk misattributing it.

What the site could safely say: that as of August 2024, both Moody's (B3) and S&P (B) rated Allied Universal in speculative-grade territory, that Moody's own stated reason was the company's leverage and acquisition-driven debt load, and that the company was around 8 times levered against EBITDA at that time, all with the date attached and a link to the source. It should not say the company is "in trouble" today, because I found no rating action or filing from the last twelve months confirming or updating that picture. This is a useful negative result: the last confirmed public data point is over a year old, and the site should say so plainly rather than imply anything current.

### Case B: a public security vendor with a recent layoff or impairment disclosure

**Rapid7, Inc.** filed an 8-K on August 7, 2026, under Item 2.05, disclosing board approval of a restructuring plan cutting the workforce by approximately 12 percent, with estimated charges of 10 to 11 million dollars, mostly severance, transition, and benefits costs, expected to land mostly in the third and fourth quarters of 2026. Read on the page: `https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001560327/000156032726000043/rp-20260807.htm` (opened 2026-09-20). Trade press coverage described this as tied to a strategic shift toward an "AI-first" platform under new leadership; that framing is the press's characterization, not language from the filing itself, so it should be kept separate if cited at all.

What the site could safely say: "Rapid7 disclosed in an SEC filing dated August 7, 2026, a restructuring plan cutting approximately 12 percent of its workforce, with estimated charges of $10 to $11 million," with a link to the filing. This is a rung 1 signal, the strongest kind, and needs no hedging beyond the filing's own words.

### Case C: a recent security shutdown or distressed sale

**PlaxidityX**, the automotive cybersecurity company formerly known as Argus (acquired by Continental in 2017 for a reported 450 million dollars), is being wound down by its parent, Aumovio (a spinoff of Continental). Aumovio laid off the remaining 80 Israeli employees around June 30, 2026, following an earlier 2025 layoff round, with the company quoted as citing "the rapid changes in the automotive industry and an increasingly challenging market environment, with slower-than-expected growth," and stating a decision to focus on "businesses with long-term value and growth potential." Read on the page: `https://www.calcalistech.com/ctechnews/article/rj3q6xzmfg` (opened 2026-09-20).

What the site could safely say: "Aumovio, the parent of automotive cybersecurity firm PlaxidityX (formerly Argus), began winding down the business around June 30, 2026, laying off its remaining 80 employees in Israel, according to [outlet], which quoted the company's own stated reasons." This is a rung 5 signal on the ladder (trade press quoting the company, not a primary press release or filing I could independently locate), so it should be labeled as reported by that outlet rather than confirmed by an Aumovio press release, unless a direct Aumovio statement can also be found and linked.
